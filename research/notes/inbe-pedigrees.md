# The three generational Inbe pedigrees (and the forgery verdict)

Found 2026-10-02. These are the only generation-by-generation accounts linking
Inbe families to their gods found so far.

## What exists

Japanese Wikipedia (忌部氏, section 忌部氏の系図とそれに対する批判) names three
documents. They were submitted in 1870 (Meiji 3) when the Jingikan sent shrines
a ten-question inquiry, and the National Diet Library holds copies:

1. **安房国忌部家系 (Awa no kuni Inbe kakei)**: the pedigree of the
   **Takayama family**, priests of **Awa Shrine** (Tateyama, Chiba). NDL
   digital pid 2538213, doi 10.11501/2538213, 75 images, public domain,
   Matsudaira family collection stamp. IIIF manifest:
   `https://dl.ndl.go.jp/api/iiif/2538213/manifest.json`.
2. **忌部岡島家系 (Inbe Okajima kakei)**: the **Okajima family**, said to be
   from **Shimotatsu-Matsubara Shrine** (Minamibōsō). NDL copy not yet located.
3. **斎部宿禰本系帳 (Inbe no Sukune honkeichō)**: the **Ono family**. NDL copy
   not yet located.

## The verdict on them

The kokugaku scholar **Kosugi Sugimura (小杉榲邨)** examined all three originals
in 1876 (Meiji 9) and wrote his findings as headnotes in red on the NDL copies.
Wikipedia quotes the headnotes in full. In summary:

- **Awa / Takayama:** the scroll's paper and handwriting looked only 40–50
  years old, though it had been made to look older. "全編すべて疑わしき"
  ("the whole thing is doubtful"). He allowed that it might have been put
  together from some genuine fragments. Of the three, he thought it **the only
  one with anything usable**, with the longest history of being handed down.
- **Okajima:** a copy written 3–5 years earlier, taken from another family's
  pedigree after the house's records burned. "実に論外" ("really out of the
  question").
- **Ono honkeichō:** made by **Suzuki Masatoshi (鈴木真年, also called Suzuki
  Toneri)**, a well-known Edo/Meiji genealogist and alleged forger. Kosugi saw
  Suzuki's own manuscript, which was the template, and found succession and
  dates deliberately rearranged to make it look independent. The head of the
  Ono family admitted the house's pedigrees had burned and this one had been
  pieced together recently.

Kosugi's conclusion was that the Okajima and Ono pedigrees were probably put
together in a hurry for the 1870 inquiry.

## What the Awa pedigree contains (first pages read)

The image numbers are NDL canvases.

- Image 3: title 安房国忌部家系, Kosugi's red headnote dated Meiji 9 April, and
  the first entry, **天日鷲命 Ame no Hiwashi**, with a narrative of Susanoo,
  the cave of Amaterasu, and so on.
- Image 5: Ame no Hiwashi is called the grandson of 神産巣日御祖大神
  (**Kamimusubi**), not of Takamimusubi as in Wikidata. The text names his
  children **大麻比古命 Ōasahiko** (also 津咋見命 Tsukuimi), **天白羽鳥命 Ame no
  Shirahatori** (also 長白羽命 Nagashiraha), and **天羽雷雄命 Ame no Hazuchio**
  (also 武羽槌命 Takehazuchi), and tells how they planted hemp and paper-mulberry and made
  the white and blue offering cloths. The format is a narrative pedigree with
  generations in sequence.

## What this means for the user's question

- **Generation-by-generation accounts do exist** for at least two Inbe lines.
  They are the Awa Shrine Takayama line from Ame no Hiwashi, and the Ono line;
  the starting god of the Ono line is not yet seen.
- **They are 19th-century documents, and a contemporary expert judged them
  forged or doubtful.** They show what the families claimed in 1870, not a
  documented ancient lineage.
- *Inference:* the FamilySearch Hamanari → Chikazane line and the genealogy
  Wikipedia cites for Fumiyama ("父：斎部伴主", from 宝賀寿男『古代氏族系譜集成』,
  1986) may come from this same family of late pedigrees. Hōga's compilation
  draws on Suzuki Masatoshi's collections. To verify.

## The god-to-human junction in the Awa pedigree (images 12–15)

Transcription so far: `genealogy/awa_inbe_kakei.csv`.

- **Image 12 is the junction.** 由布津主命 Yufutsunushi (also 阿八和氣毘古命), son
  of Ōasahiko and grandson of Ame no Hiwashi, marries **飯長姫命 Iinagahime,
  daughter of 天止美命 = Ame no Tomi**, who is Futodama's grandson. Their son is
  **訶多々主命 Katatanushi**, who served at 葛城之高岡宮 (the palace of Emperor
  Suizei). The text calls him the start of the Awa (Bōsō) Inbe: 安房忌部…之元始.
  So this pedigree joins **both** divine lines, Hiwashi on the father's side and
  Futodama on the mother's.
- **Images 12–14 give one ancestor per emperor.** Each generation is labelled
  with the palace of the emperor it served, in the order of the official list:
  Annei, Itoku, Kōshō, Kōan, Kōrei, Kōgen, Kaika, Sujin, Suinin, Keikō, Seimu,
  Chūai, Ōjin/Nintoku, … , Keitai, Ankan–Kinmei. That's 23 generations with
  almost no dates, laid out on the scaffold of the Kojiki/Nihon Shoki emperor
  list.
  - *Inference:* this is the standard pattern of a constructed pedigree. Some
    of those legendary emperors reigned for a century or more, and here each
    gets exactly one Inbe ancestor.
- **Dated people begin in the 7th–8th centuries** (image 15): 名美麻呂 d. Hakuhō
  11; 久米麻呂 d. Yōrō 4 (720); 勝麻呂 (Tenmu's reign), who founds a site for
  Takamimusubi at Sugiyama in Musashi; 義麻呂, priest of Sugiyama Shrine, d.
  Tenpyō 17 (745).
- **One link to a known court Inbe:** 勝義 Katsuyoshi is said to be "really [the
  son of] the Jingi Taifu Inbe no Sukune 色洞子". That is probably 忌部色夫知
  Inbe no Shikobuchi (Wikidata Q11490725), a real 7th–8th century official.
  This would be the only point where this pedigree touches someone in the
  chronicles, and it happens through an adoption. To verify.
- Images 16–75 continue through the Heian period (image 20: dates from Hōan
  1121 and Kenpō 1217, Minamoto no Yoritomo's arrival in Awa in 1180) to the
  16th-century Takayama heads (images 25, 45, 65). Still to transcribe.
